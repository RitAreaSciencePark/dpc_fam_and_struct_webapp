About DPCexplorer

An interactive platform for exploring DPCfam and DPCstruct protein domain classifications.

The Annotation Gap

Every organism, from a simple bacterium to a human being, depends on proteins to carry out the chemical reactions that make life possible. Understanding what a protein does, in other words, knowing its function, is therefore one of the most important goals in modern biology. The gold standard for assigning function is direct experimental characterisation. That method is reliable, but it is also slow and expensive, and it cannot keep pace with the speed of discovery.

High-throughput sequencing has deposited millions of new sequences every year. UniProtKB now holds over 245 million sequences, yet only a tiny fraction carry experimentally verified annotations. The launch of AlphaFold2 made things even more striking: reliable 3D structure predictions now exist for over 200 million proteins. The data is there. The annotations are not.

Domain Family Classification

One practical shortcut is protein domain family classification. The idea is simple: proteins in the same family share a common evolutionary origin and, usually, a similar function. If one member is well studied, that knowledge can be carefully extended to the rest. This is the logic behind Pfam, the most widely used protein family database. Pfam builds families from Multiple Sequence Alignments and profile Hidden Markov Models, which lets it detect distant relatives even when raw sequence similarity is low.

This approach works well. Expert curation improves annotation quality, and recent deep learning additions push coverage further still. But there is a fundamental limit: quality does not scale with quantity. Expert curation is inherently limited by human bandwidth. Deep learning models, however powerful, are probabilistic and always predict based on their training data.

The core problem. Only a tiny fraction of known proteins have been experimentally characterised. The vast majority exist only as sequences or predicted structures in databases: proteins without confirmed functions, without clear biological roles, and often without an assigned family.

A fully unsupervised alternative: DPCfam and DPCstruct

DPCfam and DPCstruct take a different approach. Instead of starting from curated families or training on known annotations, they start from raw pairwise similarity between proteins and let the data organise itself.

For sequences, this means BLASTp all-versus-all comparisons across 23 million UniRef50 sequences, producing roughly 55 billion alignments. For structures, the same idea is applied to 15 million AlphaFold2-predicted structures using Foldseek and MMseqs2, which translate 3D shapes into a structural alphabet for large-scale comparison.

The clustering itself is handled by the Density Peak Clustering (DPC) algorithm (Rodriguez & Laio, 2014). DPC identifies cluster centres as points with high local density and high isolation from other dense regions, no prior assumption about the number or shape of clusters needed . A two-scale design first segments individual proteins into domain-like units (primary clusters), then groups those units globally into evolutionary families called metaclusters.

Because no annotation database was consulted during clustering, the resulting metaclusters are fully independent hypotheses about evolutionary relationships. Validation results are strong: DPCfam recovers 81 % of known Pfam families; DPCstruct recovers 91 % of SCOP folds and 83 % of CATH folds. The average intra-cluster sequence identity in DPCstruct is only 34.5 %, well within the twilight zone where standard sequence-based tools become unreliable. Structure-based clustering reveals evolutionary relationships that sequence alone cannot detect.

A significant portion of metaclusters in both datasets carry no known Pfam annotation: exactly 47,002 metaclusters (33,179 in DPCfam and 13,823 in DPCstruct). These are labeled UNKNOWN in DPCexplorer. They are not errors or noise; rather, they are structurally and sequentially coherent protein families that simply have not been named yet. Some may represent novel folds, while others may be ancient families overlooked by curation-based approaches. The evidence for their biological relevance is concrete: 63 DPCfam UNKNOWN metaclusters were adopted as official new entries in Pfam release 35.0.

What DPCexplorer does

Both datasets were published on Zenodo in line with FAIR data principles. However, exploring them directly required downloading and parsing gigabyte-scale archives, including an 81 GB XML file for DPCfam. This presents a significant barrier for most researchers.

DPCexplorer removes that barrier. The raw archives were cleaned and preprocessed on the ORFEO HPC cluster, then loaded into a PostgreSQL database optimized for fast searches across millions of rows. A Django 6.0.1 backend serves this data through a responsive web interface, with no login required.

Four ways to query the database:
• DPCfam MCID (e.g., MC1): opens a sequence-based metacluster page.
• DPCstruct MCID (e.g., MC5): opens a structure-based metacluster page.
• Pfam ID (e.g., PF02990 or CL0192): displays every metacluster in both datasets that overlaps with that Pfam family or clan.
• UniProt accession (e.g., A0A182N2I3): visualises all DPCfam, DPCstruct, and Pfam domains mapped onto the full protein sequence as a domain-architecture diagram.

Each metacluster detail page is organized into four tabs. Summary displays key biological properties and Pfam annotations. Sequences lists the seed domains with UniProt links, paginated ten rows at a time. 3D Viewer: for DPCstruct, it renders AlphaFold2-predicted structures directly in the browser via PDBe-Mol*, colored by per-residue pLDDT confidence; for DPCfam, it links to the representative entry in AlphaFoldDB where available. Downloads provides one-click access to FASTA seed sequences, MSA profiles, and HMM models for DPCfam, as well as representative FASTA sequences and zipped PDB files for DPCstruct.

All preprocessed files used to build this database are openly available on Zenodo (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20159208), so any researcher can reproduce a running instance of DPCexplorer without re-running the heavy HPC preprocessing steps.

🔎 Do you have a hypothesis about an UNKNOWN metacluster?
Roughly half the metaclusters in DPCexplorer are unnamed in any existing database. They represent coherent protein families waiting to be identified. If you recognize a pattern, from structural biology, biochemistry, evolutionary genomics, or metagenomics, we want to hear from you. Open an issue on our GitHub repository or contact us directly. Every hypothesis matters.
